# About Discover the Cosmos

Discover the Cosmos lets you ask questions about space in your own words, and shows you the answer instead of only telling you. There is no account to create and nothing to set up.

## What you can ask

**Pictures from the archive.** Ask about nebulae, galaxies, planets, eclipses and more, and see pictures from NASA's Astronomy Picture of the Day archive. You get up to three at a time, and "Show more" brings more.

**Asteroids passing Earth.** See every near-Earth asteroid expected over the next 7 days, placed by how close each one comes.

**The International Space Station.** See where the station is right now and where it will be over the next 90 minutes.

You can combine them in one question, for example "Show me two nebula pictures and where the ISS is."

## What the picture archive covers

The picture archive used here covers roughly the last two years of Astronomy Picture of the Day entries and is updated regularly, so older pictures are not found. If nothing in it matches your question, you are told so.

## What it can't answer

Anything outside those three topics, such as general space trivia or who is aboard the station. It also can't answer questions about one particular asteroid, comparisons between asteroids, or one specific point on the station's path. (You can still tap a circle or a dot inside an illustration to see its details.) When a question is outside what the product can answer, it tells you so and explains what it can answer instead.

## One question at a time

Each question is answered on its own. Discover the Cosmos doesn't remember your earlier questions, so "tell me more about that one" won't work. Name what you mean instead, such as the title of a picture. Your earlier answers stay in the conversation as chips that you can reopen for as long as this tab is open. Closing the tab clears them.

## How to read the illustrations

**Asteroids.** Earth is on the left, and the farther right an asteroid sits, the farther away it passes. Distances are drawn on a compressed scale so that near and far asteroids fit on one screen, and they are given in lunar distances (one lunar distance is the distance from Earth to the Moon). Height has no meaning: asteroids are spread out only so that every one stays visible. Circle sizes show size groups, not exact sizes, and neither Earth nor the asteroids are drawn to a common scale. A red circle with a ring is a "potentially hazardous" asteroid. That is NASA's classification, based on an asteroid's size and orbit. It does not mean an impact is expected. Drag to explore, and tap a circle for details.

**The space station.** The dots show where the station will be every 10 minutes over the next 90 minutes, shaded for day or night. The drawing is a snapshot taken at the time shown and does not update on its own; use "Ask again" for a fresh one. The station's height above Earth is exaggerated so that it can be seen. The Moon is drawn to give a sense of scale: the station is roughly a thousandth of the way to it.

## Where the information comes from

- **Pictures:** NASA's Astronomy Picture of the Day. Every picture is credited to its owner on its card, with a link to the original entry. Many pictures are credited to third-party photographers and institutions, who keep the rights to them.
- **Asteroids:** near-Earth object data from NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, provided through NASA's open data service.
- **Space station position:** the wheretheiss.at service, which is independent of NASA.

Names, dates, distances and positions come straight from these sources, and every answer shows where it came from.
